Net profit tells you what is left after everything; EBITDA tells you whether the **operations** make money before financing choices, tax and accounting for old assets get in the way. Lenders, investors and buyers of small businesses quote it constantly ("we'll pay 4× EBITDA"), and MSME loan appraisals lean on it. This calculator computes EBITDA and EBITDA margin from either a profit-and-loss statement (net profit plus interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) or from revenue and operating expenses, and shows EBIT alongside.

## What EBITDA is

Earnings **B**efore **I**nterest, **T**axes, **D**epreciation and **A**mortisation. Starting from net profit (PAT): add back income tax, interest on loans, depreciation on tangible assets and amortisation of intangibles. Starting from revenue: subtract cost of goods sold and cash operating expenses (salaries, rent, marketing, utilities), but not depreciation, interest or tax. EBITDA margin = EBITDA ÷ revenue.

Example: revenue ₹1.2 crore, PAT ₹12 lakh, interest ₹4 lakh, tax ₹4.5 lakh, depreciation ₹6 lakh, amortisation ₹0.5 lakh → EBITDA **₹27 lakh**, margin 22.5%; EBIT ₹20.5 lakh.

## Why it matters for a small business

- **Loan appraisal**: banks look at EBITDA ÷ interest (interest coverage) and debt ÷ EBITDA. Coverage below 2× or debt above 3–4× EBITDA makes lenders nervous; run scenarios with the [EMI calculator](/tools/emi-calculator).
- **Valuation**: small businesses in India change hands at roughly 3–6× EBITDA depending on sector and growth; a ₹27 lakh EBITDA business might be valued at ₹80 lakh to ₹1.6 crore.
- **Comparing branches or product lines** that carry different asset ages and loans — EBITDA strips out those differences.
- **Tracking operations monthly** without waiting for the CA's year-end depreciation entries.

## How to use the calculator

1. Choose the starting point: **from PAT** (add-backs) or **from revenue** (subtract operating costs).
2. Enter the figures from your P&L — use the depreciation from the [depreciation calculator](/tools/depreciation-calculator) if you have not yet booked it.
3. Read EBITDA, EBITDA margin, EBIT and, if you enter interest and debt, the interest coverage and debt-to-EBITDA ratios.
4. Compare periods to see whether operating profitability is improving independent of loan or tax changes.

## The limits of EBITDA

- It ignores **capital expenditure**: a transport business with an EBITDA of ₹30 lakh that must replace ₹25 lakh of trucks every few years is not earning ₹30 lakh.
- It ignores **working capital**: growing receivables can starve cash while EBITDA looks healthy — watch [inventory turnover](/tools/inventory-turnover) and collection days.
- It is not cash; GST, TDS and advance tax are paid from cash. Pair it with [net profit margin](/tools/net-profit-margin) for the real bottom line.
- "Adjusted EBITDA" with owner salary added back is fine for valuation but say so explicitly.

## FAQ

### Is EBITDA the same as operating profit?

Close but not identical: operating profit (EBIT) is after depreciation and amortisation; EBITDA adds those back. Both exclude interest and tax.

### Should I add back the owner's salary?

For valuation, buyers often compute "seller's discretionary earnings" by adding back owner compensation above market. For lending and internal tracking, keep a market salary as a cost.

### What is a good EBITDA margin?

Trading businesses run 5–12%, manufacturing 12–25%, services and software 20–40%. Trend matters more than the level.

### Does GST affect EBITDA?

No. GST collected and paid is a pass-through and should be excluded from revenue and costs. Only non-creditable GST that becomes part of an expense affects it.

### Which figure do banks use for loan eligibility?

Usually cash accruals (PAT + depreciation) and EBITDA-based coverage ratios, along with turnover and collateral. Showing both EBITDA and PAT in your proposal helps.
