"Sales went from ₹40 lakh to ₹1.1 crore in four years" sounds like 175% growth — but the number that lets you compare it with a mutual fund, a competitor or a business plan is the **compound annual growth rate**: 28.8% a year. CAGR is the single rate that, applied every year, takes the starting value to the ending value over the period. This calculator computes it from any two values and a time span (in years, months or exact dates), and also runs it backwards: what a value becomes at a given CAGR, or how long a value takes to reach a target.

## The formula

CAGR = (Ending value ÷ Beginning value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1.

₹40,00,000 → ₹1,10,00,000 over 4 years: (2.75)^(0.25) − 1 = **28.8%**. Reversed: ₹40 lakh at 28.8% for 4 years = ₹1.1 crore. Doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ rate (the rule of 72): at 12%, about 6 years.

CAGR smooths the path — a business that grew 60% one year and shrank 10% the next is described by the same CAGR as a steady one — so it says nothing about volatility, only about the endpoints.

## How to use the calculator

1. Enter the beginning and ending values — revenue, profit, customer count, an investment, a property price.
2. Enter the period: years (decimals allowed), or pick start and end dates for an exact span.
3. Read the CAGR and the year-by-year implied path; download it as CSV.
4. Switch to **future value** or **time to target** mode for planning.
5. Compare: paste two series (say, your sales vs your category's growth) to see relative performance.

## Where CAGR is the right metric

- **Investments**: mutual fund fact sheets report CAGR for 3-, 5- and 10-year periods; the [SIP calculator](/tools/sip-calculator) projects forward with it, and for irregular cashflows XIRR is the more precise cousin.
- **Business plans and pitch decks**: a "₹2 crore in year 3" plan implies a CAGR that should be defensible — the [revenue growth rate calculator](/tools/revenue-growth) gives the period-over-period figures underneath.
- **Property**: ₹45 lakh flat bought in 2016 sold for ₹95 lakh in 2026 = 7.75% CAGR — before stamp duty, maintenance and tax, often less impressive than the headline.
- **Loans and inflation**: inflation at 6% is a CAGR on your costs; a supplier contract with a 5% annual escalation is a CAGR on price.
- **Headcount, stores, users**: any metric measured at intervals.

## Interpreting the number

- Compare like with like: a 5-year CAGR against a 5-year benchmark; nominal against nominal (or subtract inflation for real growth).
- Small bases exaggerate: growing from 10 customers to 100 is a 58% CAGR over five years but tells you little about the next five.
- Negative CAGR is valid — a decline from ₹80 lakh to ₹60 lakh over 3 years is −9.1% a year.
- For returns, remember tax: equity long-term gains at 12.5%, and slab rates on interest, so a 12% pre-tax CAGR may be 10.5% post-tax — the [income tax calculator](/tools/income-tax) shows the slab.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between CAGR and average annual growth?

The arithmetic average of yearly growth rates overstates compounding. Growth of +50% then −50% averages 0% but leaves you at 75% of the start; CAGR (−13.4%) reflects the actual outcome.

### Can CAGR be used for less than a year?

Yes, using fractional years (6 months = 0.5), though annualising a short period exaggerates both good and bad results.

### Is CAGR the same as XIRR?

XIRR handles multiple cashflows on different dates (like SIP instalments); CAGR assumes a single start and end value. For a lumpsum investment they match.

### What is a good CAGR for a small business?

Sustained 15–25% revenue CAGR is strong for an established small business; early-stage companies grow faster off small bases. Profit CAGR matters more than revenue CAGR.

### How do I calculate CAGR in Excel?

=(End/Start)^(1/Years)−1, or use the RRI function: =RRI(years, start, end).
